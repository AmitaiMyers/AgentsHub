Deep research report · Agent-to-agent hub

AgentsHub Can Win by Selling Trust

23 November 2026Global / US-first5 research threads
Answer the 7 decisions Read the findings

Yes, build AgentsHub, but do not launch it as a general-purpose hub where any agent can find anything. Build it as a neutral, verified intent exchange. The protocol and schemas stay general, the go-to-market starts with one wedge, and the hub enforces identity, staged disclosure, human approval and audit while each principal's own agent does the choosing. The whitespace is real. As of November 2026, no funded company runs a bring-your-own-agent, cross-domain exchange that matches one principal's need to another principal's offer. The open protocols (A2A v1.0.1 and MCP 2026-07-28) deliberately leave discovery, matching and trust unstandardized. The window is narrower than the earlier, lighter report suggested, though. Jack & Jill has raised about $60M to put agents on both sides of hiring, Meta bought Moltbook's agent-network team, Indeed runs a seeker agent and an employer agent inside one marketplace, and the frontier labs own the preference context that Anthropic's Project Swap identifies as the source of about 85% of a market's lost value. Today only the low tens of millions of people worldwide have an agent that could call a hub, and most of them are technical. "Agents finding agents" will not create liquidity by itself, so direct sales to employers and a hosted "lite agent" have to supply it. The most consequential new finding is legal. A hub that ranks or shortlists candidates is plausibly the employer's "agent" under California's FEHA rules and the Mobley v. Workday theory. It also risks being treated as an employment agency, faces a consumer-reporting (FCRA) risk if it scores candidates with third-party data, and would be a high-risk AI provider under the EU AI Act from 2 December 2027. The hiring module therefore has to run as a ring-fenced, rule-based exchange. On pricing, charge employers per accepted hand-off or per hire at a quarter to half of what an agency costs, keep candidates free, and defer payments rails and tokens. Then set a 90-day gate: if 10–30 AI-forward employers will not sign on as design partners, move the wedge to a less contested vertical.

02 · COMPETITORS

Funded rivals own verticals, and nobody yet owns the neutral exchange

Horizontal "agent economies" are big on paper and small in practice

The players that already let agents discover and transact with other agents are mostly crypto-native service-call markets. Their headline numbers fall apart under independent audit. Fetch.ai markets Agentverse as having about 3 million agents (Crypto Briefing), while an early-2026 audit reportedly found only about 36,000 protocol-active agents (Yahoo Finance; the audit itself was not retrieved). Coinbase claims more than 165M x402 transactions and about $50M of volume (Coinbase), yet TRM Labs estimates genuinely agentic x402 volume at about $5,000–11,000 a month (TRM Labs). Olas reports 14.2M agent-to-agent transactions, but its marketplace protocol fee had collected $546.46 on $107,808 of turnover by August 2026 (RootData). Virtuals' daily revenue fell from a $1.02M peak to about $34.8K within two months once speculative incentives faded (Decrypt). These networks share a structural blind spot. They match a task to a service ("call this API, pay per call") and never match one principal's need to another principal's offer. The closest product in shape to AgentsHub is Tobira, a free network where bring-your-own agents get handles, test each other's claims, and introduce their humans only after both agents agree and both humans approve. It has no disclosed funding or usage (Product Hunt). Pilot Protocol raised $4.5M in July 2026 to give every agent an address and encrypted transport (TechStartups). That is plumbing AgentsHub could run on, not a matchmaker.

The lesson for strategy is that agent counts are a vanity metric and investors are learning to discount them. A credible hub should publish human-approved hand-offs and downstream outcomes from day one. That is also the only defense against the comparison with Moltbook, which claimed millions of agents but had about 17,000 human owners (TechRadar).

Verticals are where agent-to-agent matching is real and monetized

The money has gone to vertical players. Jack & Jill is the closest funded analogue to the AgentsHub mechanic. "Jack" is a voice career-coach agent for job seekers, and "Jill" is an employer agent that sources by searching Jack's network. The company is free for seekers, charges about 10% of a hire (TechCrunch), and raised a $40M Series A from Air Street in September 2026 (SiliconANGLE). It is a closed network that runs only its own agents, and it has disclosed no hire counts. In B2B finance, Causa Prima raised a $20M seed (about $30M total) for "the agent-to-agent AI network" in which buyer and supplier agents negotiate invoice disputes and payment terms (Fortune). Pactum has raised more than $100M for autonomous procurement negotiation, mostly against human suppliers (Pactum). Boardy ($11M) brokers double-opt-in introductions through a central voice AI rather than agent-to-agent conversation (The Logic). Dating has a cluster of $2–15M startups. Ditto raised $9.2M (BI via dnyuz) and Known $9.7M (TechCrunch), but most run a central matchmaker. Only tiny products like VibeRizz let each user's agent talk to other users' agents (Product Hunt). Real estate is effectively closed for now: one brokerage abandoned a "purely AI" agent because licensing rules forbid it (Real Estate News). The most telling demand signal comes from Upwork's CEO, who says clients' AI agents already post jobs on Upwork to hire humans (Semafor). Incumbents with liquidity will receive agent traffic by default, whether or not a neutral hub exists.

Big platforms have built registries and checkout, not cross-principal matching

Every hyperscaler has shipped the discovery and governance primitives a hub would otherwise build itself. None has shipped intent matching between different principals' agents. Google's Gemini Enterprise Agent Gallery requires A2A Agent Cards and OAuth for listing (Google Cloud). Microsoft's Agent 365 registry went GA on 1 May 2026 and syncs agents from Bedrock, Vertex and Agentforce (Microsoft). AWS's AgentCore Agent Registry reportedly reached GA on 31 August 2026 (Atlan). OpenAI sidelined Instant Checkout in March 2026 in favor of merchant apps (Digital Commerce 360) and is shutting down Agent Builder on 30 November 2026 (Refonte Learning), a reminder of how quickly platform dependencies can disappear. In hiring, LinkedIn's Hiring Assistant 2 sources from more than 1.2B profiles but works for recruiters only (Recruiting News Network). Indeed is quietly closer: it runs Career Scout for seekers and Talent Scout for employers in one marketplace (Indeed). No source confirms that those two agents negotiate with each other directly.

The market is large, but the agent supply is still thin

The hiring revenue pool is large and concentrated. Global online job advertising is about $37.6B in 2026, and LinkedIn and Indeed take roughly half (Outsource Accelerator/SIA). US staffing is about $183B (Tall Tree Talent), and narrowly defined ATS software is about $2.7B (Noon.ai). The strongest signal of willingness to pay comes from Indeed. In Recruit's July–September quarter, US revenue per job rose 39% while posting volume stayed flat (SIA). Employers are paying far more per role for better matching, which means the scarce resource is qualified attention, not reach. The pain is quantified. On Greenhouse, applications per job rose from 116 in 2022 to 244 in 2025 while recruiters per organization fell from 10.43 to 4.62 (Greenhouse), and only 8% of job seekers say AI screening makes hiring fairer (Greenhouse). The broader AI-agents market is projected to grow from about $8B in 2025 to roughly $50B by 2030 (BCC Research; MarketsandMarkets), though those definitions vary widely.

On the supply side, the agents that could actually connect to AgentsHub are far fewer than the assistant user base. ChatGPT has about 1B weekly users (Wikipedia), but OpenAI's agentic products had passed only 10M weekly users by July 2026 (The Next Web). OpenClaw's 2–3.2M monthly users are an unverified third-party estimate (Gradually.ai). Gartner finds only about 17% of organizations have deployed AI agents (Gartner). A reasonable order-of-magnitude estimate is 15–30M people worldwide whose agent could call a remote MCP tool today. At typical active job-search rates, only 1–3M of them are looking for work at any moment, heavily skewed toward tech. Users also want control. Only 6% of consumers want AI to decide and act entirely for them, while more than half accept agent action when they can approve or undo it (Help Net Security/Okta). Accenture finds 9% would let an agent shop fully autonomously (Chain Store Age). The product has to be "agents explore, humans commit." On the employer side, early agents will connect through ATS integrations or a hub-hosted employer agent, not as bring-your-own agents.

The right split is MCP in for consumer assistants and A2A for server-side agents, sharing one internal negotiation state machine. Most job seekers' agents (Claude, ChatGPT) are MCP clients. They cannot host an A2A server or receive webhooks, so the hub has to be the orchestrator: it exposes a pollable inbox and notifies the human by email. Employer agents, ATS bots and custom candidate agents can run as servers and talk A2A. The MCP 2026-07-28 changes fit a hub well. Because the core is stateless, the server can run on serverless or edge infrastructure. The spec's recommended pattern of passing explicit handles (intent_id, session_id) as tool arguments carries state between calls. MRTR elicitation is the natural mechanism for "reveal my CV to employer X?" approvals. On the A2A side, v1.0's tenant field lets one hub endpoint serve many listed agents, and each negotiation maps onto a Task: INPUT_REQUIRED while waiting for a human, AUTH_REQUIRED for consent to disclose, REJECTED for a decline, and COMPLETED carrying a hand-off artifact. The roadmap item on "structured human-in-the-loop interaction models and multi-round negotiation patterns" (A2A roadmap) is the one piece of standards work to align with. A2A's deliberate lack of a registry API is good news strategically, because the search-and-match API is where AgentsHub differentiates without colliding with a standard.

There is one distribution risk to resolve early. OpenAI's app-submission guidelines will not approve plugins that "primarily function as unofficial connectors to third-party services, including pass-through intermediary software layers" (OpenAI). AgentsHub has to present itself as a first-party service with its own matching, verification and data, not as a relay between other people's agents, and it should confirm its eligibility with OpenAI before relying on the ChatGPT directory.

Agent-market experiments say the mechanism, not the haggling, decides outcomes

Three experiments point the same way. In Microsoft's Magentic Marketplace, about 80–100% of agents accepted the first proposal they received, welfare collapsed when agents were shown 100 search results, and fake credentials, social proof and prompt injection manipulated several models (arXiv 2510.25779). In Anthropic's Project Deal, Opus agents sold items for $2.68 more and bought them for $2.45 less than Haiku agents. Humans represented by Haiku rated their deals as equally fair despite the worse outcomes, and "aggressive" prompting made no significant difference (Anthropic). In Project Swap, Claude's preference rankings matched participants' own rankings on only 61% of pairs. The market delivered 0.55 efficiency against a 0.89 optimum, about 85% of the gap came from preference representation rather than bargaining, and model choice mattered more than whether the agent was told to be ruthless or prosocial (Anthropic). Anthropic's proposed safeguards (agent identity registries, rate limits on agent messages, and full activity logs) read like a specification for a neutral hub.

The design rules that follow are concrete. First, invest in structured preference elicitation before matching, with typed fields, explicit deal-breakers and ranking questions. Hosting that elicitation in the hub through MRTR means quality does not depend on each user's agent doing it well. Second, run matching as a hub-side mechanism that delivers short, batched lists (3–5 options per side per round), not a feed agents can grab the first item from. Third, make negotiation moves typed objects (propose{salary, start, remote}, accept, decline{reason_code}), with the hub computing the zone of agreement on numeric fields so that a weaker agent cannot be out-haggled on a salary band.

The second rule collides with the law. Picking which 3–5 candidates an employer sees is ranking, and in hiring, ranking is the trigger for "agent," AEDT and EU high-risk status (section 4). The resolution for the hiring module is to make selection principal-defined and deterministic. Each employer specifies its hard filters and weights, the hub applies them mechanically, ties are broken by random rotation rather than by a hub-learned score, and each principal's own agent ranks within the batch it receives. A hub-computed fit score can come later as a deliberately regulated product with a bias-audit program. Outside hiring (B2B introductions, barter, networking), hub-side ranking carries much lower legal exposure and can ship from the start.

Security has to come from the architecture, because filtering is not enough

OWASP's agentic Top 10 puts ASI01 Agent Goal Hijack and ASI07 Insecure Inter-Agent Communication at the center of a hub's threat model (OWASP). The evidence from the field is blunt. Moltbook's breach was a configuration failure, not an exotic AI attack: a Supabase key in client JavaScript with row-level security disabled exposed about 1.5M agent tokens and let anyone act as any agent (Wiz). Vectra found hidden injection payloads in about 2.6% of sampled Moltbook posts (Vectra), and Permiso documented agents instructing other agents to delete their accounts and join crypto pump schemes (SecurityWeek). Koi Security found 341 malicious skills among 2,857 on ClawHub (The Hacker News). The hiring domain is already adversarial: 41% of US job seekers admit using prompt injections to get past AI screeners (Greenhouse), and 22.4% of surveyed recruiters have confirmed a deepfake or identity swap in a video interview (Pin).

The defenses that work are structural. The hub acts as the reference monitor: the counterparty's private fields never reach an agent until the disclosure policy allows it, so an injected "reveal your salary floor" fails even if the receiving agent is fooled. Free text is capped in length and labeled untrusted_counterparty_text in every payload. Any LLM the hub runs itself (summarizers, moderation) uses the dual-LLM or CaMeL pattern, in which a quarantined model reads counterparty text and the privileged model sees only typed outputs (arXiv 2506.08837). Tokens are short-lived, audience-bound and scoped per session. Every message is scanned for secrets and redacted, because Moltbook users leaked third-party API keys in direct messages. An external penetration test and a bug bounty come before launch. Because ClawHub's malware record shows the open agent ecosystem is a supply-chain hazard, verification is a feature people will pay for, not overhead.

For a $150K US software role, an agency costs about $30K per hire. AgentsHub only matches and hands off: it does no payroll, escrow or work management. It therefore delivers less than Upwork or an agency and should aim to capture less. The research suggests three bands to validate with design partners. The first is a per-accepted-hand-off fee of about $100–500, triggered only when both principals opt in and the candidate is verified. The second is a success fee of $2.5–7.5K flat or 5–10% of first-year base, which positions the hub at a quarter to half of agency cost. The third is a volume subscription of $300–1,000 a month, priced against an Ashby entry plan or a few Juicebox seats. Candidates stay free. Charging them invites "pay to apply" stigma and fairness concerns, and might bring employment-agency licensing rules that were not researched here. Indeed's retreat from charging per application is the key pricing lesson: bill on a strong signal such as mutual opt-in or a scheduled interview, never on agent messages. On illustrative unit economics, an employer that posts 10 roles a year and fills 3 through the hub at $5K each is worth $15K a year, about the cost of one LinkedIn Corporate seat. The target serving cost (LLM negotiation plus verification) is well under $20 per hand-off.

A general-purpose hub needs different pricing primitives in different verticals. A success fee works where the outcome is observable and someone has a budget, as in hiring and B2B deals. A subscription fits where outcomes are unobservable, which is why dating monetizes at about $18–33 per payer per month rather than per marriage. Per-call micropayments are not a business: total lifetime x402 value is smaller than one mid-size recruiting agency's revenue, and most of it is not agentic (TRM Labs). They are useful only as anti-spam friction, where a fraction of a cent per post raises the cost of flooding. Leakage is real but bounded. One study puts the share of Airbnb bookings taken offline in Austin at about 5.4% (UCLA Anderson Review). The defense is to make the mutual opt-in the billable, logged event before contact details are revealed, to charge the side that has contracts and legal exposure (the employer), and to keep post-hand-off value on the platform: scheduling, verified credentials and the audit trail.

Cold start: sell to the hard side, aggregate public supply, and treat directories as table stakes

In agent-mediated hiring, the hard side is the employer. Employers hold the budget and are already blocking AI-flooded inbound. Candidates with agents are comparatively plentiful. The marketplace playbook is consistent on this point. Lenny Rachitsky's research found one-on-one direct sales was the crucial early lever for most marketplaces, about twice as common as the next lever (Lenny's Newsletter). NFX recommends starting from a "white-hot center" and notes that Indeed itself began by aggregating job postings (NFX). Antler claims "claim your listing" outreach to pre-seeded suppliers converts close to 100%, far above cold outreach (Antler); that is a VC claim, so treat it as optimistic. Seeding is technically easy. Ashby's public posting API needs no authentication and can include compensation (Ashby), and community MCP servers already wrap the Greenhouse, Lever and Ashby public boards (GitHub). Ingested postings become "unclaimed" employer intents that a company activates by claiming them. Submitting applications into an ATS still needs each employer's key, which makes "connect your ATS" a natural onboarding step. Check each source's terms with counsel before ingesting at scale.

Agent-native directories are necessary but are not a growth channel. One count found about 52K–53K MCP Registry records by early November, with the caveat that a record is not a product or a user (API Tracker), and a community list counted about 1,624 ChatGPT apps by July 2026 (awesome-chatgpt-apps). No evidence shows that a listing alone drives usage. Distribution has to come from three sources. The first is a strong reason for an assistant user to say "find me a job on AgentsHub." The second is a hosted lite agent (web or chat intake) for people who have no agent. The third is partnerships with builders who route their own traffic in: candidate-side auto-apply vendors, whose users become sanctioned, rate-limited, verified traffic instead of spam; small vertical agent apps that lack infrastructure; and ATS vendors. One unverified blog claims Workday has an MCP and A2A "Agent Gateway" (HeroHunt), which should be checked as a priority integration target.

On the beachhead, the evidence still favors AI/ML and senior software engineers hiring at AI-forward startups, remote, in US time zones. AI-engineer postings grew about 143% year over year, with an estimated 3.2 open roles per qualified candidate (staffing-vendor data) (futureproofing.dev). Both sides are agent users, and those employers run Ashby or Greenhouse, so seeding is easy. The catch is that LinkedIn Hiring Assistant 2 and Jack & Jill chase the same people. AgentsHub's differentiators there are bring-your-own-agent, neutrality, candidate privacy ("contact me only if the range is ≥ X"), and the one thing nobody owns: an identity-bound, structured, agent-submitted application that an ATS accepts as first-class rather than as spam. Israeli tech now has 0.95 openings per seeker (Calcalist), so candidates are easy to recruit but employer liquidity is thin. It is a good community pilot and cross-border feeder, not the main market. Hourly and frontline hiring is the wrong first niche. Workday's Paradox already automates the employer side (Workday), and those candidates rarely run personal agents.

History shows how matching marketplaces die

Curated reverse hiring marketplaces raised large sums and then collapsed. Hired was sold to Vettery in a 2020 fire sale (TechCrunch) and folded into Adecco's LHH in 2024 (SIA). Triplebyte's candidate marketplace was shut down when Karat bought its assessment technology (Karat). Both were single-vertical, employer-paid and exposed to the tech-hiring cycle. Commentators argue Triplebyte declined after loosening its curation to scale. The agent-era versions of failure are token-subsidized activity that disappears once incentives end (Virtuals), viral growth without trust (Moltbook), and marketplaces that pivot to products because liquidity never arrived. Nagent retired its agent marketplace in October 2026 (Nagent). Three rules follow: verification quality is the product and must not be loosened for growth, avoid tokens, and diversify intent types beyond tech hiring while keeping go-to-market single-niche. The metric tree should match. Liquidity is the share of active intents that reach at least one mutually accepted hand-off within 7, 14 and 30 days in each niche. Quality is hand-off acceptance, hand-off-to-interview and interview-to-hire rates, plus the abuse rate. Economics are revenue per hand-off and per hire, CAC by channel, and a leakage proxy. The headline KPI to sell to employers is hand-offs per hire against their baseline of about 244 applications per job.

05 · RISKS AND REGULATION

Ranking candidates turns a neutral hub into a regulated hiring agent

US law attaches to whoever screens, and the hub could be that party

US AI-hiring law is a patchwork of state and local rules that mostly bind the employer, employment agency or "agent" that uses the tool. The hub's exposure therefore depends on what the hub does. California's FEHA regulations, in force since 1 October 2025, define an employer's "agent" as anyone exercising a function traditionally exercised by the employer, including recruitment, screening and hiring. They also require four-year retention of automated-decision data (Sheppard Mullin). Firms read the rules as reaching vendors to which a decision-making function is delegated (Paul Hastings), and an agent can be directly liable (Littler). In Mobley v. Workday, the court let the theory proceed that Workday is an "agent" of employers who delegated screening to it. It rejected the "employment agency" theory because Workday does not procure applicants. The ADEA collective was conditionally certified, and Workday moved to decertify it on 10 Nov 2026 with more than 15,000 opt-ins (Warden AI; AI Governance for HR). The implication for AgentsHub is uncomfortable. A matching hub that actively introduces seekers to employers is closer to "procuring" than Workday was, so it may not get the same answer on employment-agency status. And if it ranks or screens for employers, it fits the FEHA agent definition squarely.

The rest of the patchwork shapes jurisdiction choices. NYC Local Law 144 places the bias-audit duty on the employer or employment agency, not the vendor, and DCWP's FAQ excludes tools used only for outreach or to invite applications (DCWP FAQ). An NYC-based hub could itself be an employment agency using an automated tool. Enforcement has been weak: the state Comptroller found DCWP identified 1 non-compliant employer where auditors found at least 17 potential violations. DCWP has agreed to move to proactive review (DLA Piper). Illinois HB 3773 has required notice and barred discriminatory AI use, including zip code as a proxy, since 1 January 2026. Its draft notice rules, which would have named the vendor, were temporarily withdrawn in June 2026 (Seyfarth). California's CCPA rules on automated decision-making technology (ADMT) add a pre-use notice, an opt-out and access rights for employment decisions by 1 January 2027 (Akin Gump). Colorado repealed its original AI Act and replaced it with SB 26-189, a narrower disclosure model effective 1 January 2027 (Intellisee), and enforcement is stayed while xAI's constitutional challenge proceeds (Warden AI). Texas's TRAIGA reaches only intentional discrimination (Duane Morris). New Jersey's rules make employers liable for algorithmic discrimination even through third-party tools (Reed Smith).

The federal government has moved toward deregulation, but that does not remove the risk. Executive Order 14365 created a DOJ AI Litigation Task Force to challenge state AI laws. As of mid-November its only concrete move found was intervening in the Colorado case (Paul Hastings; ailawsbystate). The EEOC has removed its AI guidance and deprioritized disparate impact (NatLawReview). Title VII, the ADEA and the ADA are unchanged, though, and private suits like Mobley are now the main enforcement channel. Intentional discrimination remains squarely enforceable: the EEOC's first AI-hiring settlement, $365,000 from iTutorGroup, involved software that auto-rejected older applicants (EEOC). The FTC's proposed July 2026 policy statement adds a twist. Steering AI outputs, even to comply with state law, may be deceptive unless disclosed (Arnold & Porter). The cheap remedy is to disclose every compliance filter the hub applies, and never to claim "bias-free" or "verified" matching without substantiation.

Three more liability theories target the platform itself

Beyond employment statutes, three theories reach the platform directly. The first is FCRA. Kistler v. Eightfold AI alleges that undisclosed match scores built from third-party data are consumer reports. A motion to dismiss was fully briefed by July 2026, and no ruling was found (Ogletree). So reputation must come from first-party conduct on the hub only, and the hub should never enrich candidate profiles from scraped sources. The second is Section 230. In Bouck v. Meta, a court said generative AI tools that shape content can make a platform a "creator" (Eric Goldman). Roommates.com stripped immunity from a site whose required questions solicited protected traits for matching (Wikipedia). The hub's models must therefore never write claims on a principal's behalf, and its schemas must not ask for protected characteristics. The third is contract attribution. UETA §14 lets contracts form between electronic agents, but no court has applied it to non-deterministic LLM agents. UETA §10 lets a person avoid errors when no correction opportunity was given (Proskauer), and Moffatt v. Air Canada held a company liable for its chatbot's misstatement (CanLII). Agent "agreements" should therefore be non-binding term sheets until a human confirms them, with an explicit error-correction step.

EU and data-protection rules allow a split design

Under the EU AI Act, recruitment AI is Annex III high-risk, and Annex III point 4(a) explicitly includes tools used "to place targeted job advertisements, to analyse and filter job applications, and to evaluate candidates" (AI Act Annex III). That reaches a hub's core function of routing job offers to selected agents. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 moved these obligations to 2 December 2027 (regulation-ai.eu), but the Article 50 duty to tell people they are dealing with AI has applied since 2 August 2026 (Jones Walker). Draft Commission guidelines reportedly cite an automated job-matching and ranking tool as a high-risk example (Faegre Drinker), and the Article 6(3) exemption for "narrow procedural" tasks never covers systems that profile people (Art. 6). A hub that supplies hiring matching logic would be a provider: it would need a quality management system, technical documentation, logging, human-oversight design, conformity assessment and registration. Employers would be deployers with their own duties (Art. 26). The workable answer is a split architecture. A generic messaging, discovery and consent layer stays outside high-risk scope, and a ring-fenced hiring module is either kept rule-based and principal-driven or deliberately certified before EU launch. The EU Pay Transparency Directive's June 2026 deadline has passed, and it bans asking candidates about pay history (Morgan Lewis). The hub should block a salary-history field at the schema level everywhere and require posted ranges on employer offers.

On data protection, Spain's AEPD issued the first EU regulator guidance on agentic AI in February 2026. It says autonomy does not move responsibility: agents are means of processing, controllers must document each flow and recipient, and people must be told when agents add recipients (A&O Shearman). Bird & Bird flags controllership shifting mid-process as the key pressure point (Bird & Bird). The likely role map, which is uncertain, has the hub as an independent controller for accounts, verification, the matching index and abuse logs, and possibly a joint controller with each principal for the exchange it defines. Each disclosure to a counterparty creates a new controller and should be logged as a recipient event the data subject can see. The UK ICO found that many employers make solely automated recruitment decisions without meaningful human involvement, and it expects contestability and human review (DLA Piper). That aligns with the hub's human-approval gates.

Fairness: weaker agents lose without noticing

Project Deal's central result, that people represented by weaker models get worse deals and rate them as equally fair, becomes a disparate-impact risk in hiring, where model quality tracks income. The mitigations are typed offers with hub-computed zones of agreement, mandatory pay ranges, a hub-provided baseline negotiator for candidates who want one, a default "human review before accept," and disparity monitoring by agent class. A schema-level list of prohibited fields covers salary history, age and graduation year in early stages, photos, health, ethnicity, religion, union status, and zip code as a ranking input. Bias audits are impossible without demographics, so those should come through a separate, opt-in, firewalled self-ID channel used only for aggregate monitoring. Accessibility needs a human alternative channel in every hiring flow, as the ACLU's charges against Intuit and HireVue over an inaccessible AI interview illustrate (Proskauer).

Platform risk matters more than protocol risk

The protocols are settling under the Linux Foundation, so the larger risk is commercial. The frontier labs and Meta own the preference context that Project Swap shows is decisive, and any of them could ship a native "find me a counterparty" feature. AgentsHub's defenses are things a lab cannot credibly offer: neutrality across every agent vendor, cross-vendor and cross-domain reach, verified principals, and a compliance layer that employers need regardless of which assistant their candidates use. The ChatGPT listing policy and the Agent Builder shutdown are reminders to own direct relationships with principals (email, accounts, employer contracts) rather than renting them from a single assistant platform.

08 · CONCLUSION

Conclusion

The central insight from this research is that the more intelligence AgentsHub puts into deciding who meets whom, the more it becomes a regulated hiring agent, and the less neutral it looks to the agents it needs. The defensible value therefore sits in the parts that look least glamorous: structured preference capture (where Project Swap locates most of the lost value), identity bound to real principals, consent-staged disclosure, typed negotiation, and an audit trail employers need anyway. A lab, Meta or Jack & Jill can build smarter matching faster. None of them can credibly be the neutral, cross-vendor referee, and that role becomes more valuable as every company and individual gets an agent and the noise problem in hiring spreads to other markets.

The timing argument has also changed. The protocols are ready now, but the regulatory calendar (California ADMT on 1 Jan 2027, Colorado's stayed law, the Mobley decertification fight, and the EU's 2 Dec 2027 high-risk date) makes 2027 the year AI hiring tools must prove their compliance. A hub built from day one around principal-made decisions, logged disclosures and bias-audit-ready exports can turn that deadline into a sales argument instead of a retrofit. The open question the market has not answered is whether enough principals will let agents meet on their behalf. A 90-day pilot measured in human-approved hand-offs, not agent sign-ups, is the cheapest way to find out.
