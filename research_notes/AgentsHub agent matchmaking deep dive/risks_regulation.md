## Q1. US AI-in-hiring laws: current status, and is the hub an "employment agency", "agent" or AEDT vendor?

### Takeaway
US hiring-AI law is a patchwork of state and local notice, anti-discrimination and record rules, and almost all of it attaches to the **employer / employment agency / "agent"** that *uses* the tool. The hub's biggest classification risk is **California's FEHA "agent" definition** and the *Mobley* "agent" theory. If the hub ranks, filters or scores candidates for employers, it can be treated as the employer's agent and held directly liable. If it only routes mutually-declared intents and leaves selection to each principal, its exposure is much lower.

### Cited Findings
**NYC Local Law 144 (AEDT)**
- State Comptroller audit (Report 2024-N-6, released 2 Dec 2025, covering Jul 2023–Jun 2025) found DCWP enforcement "ineffective." DCWP surveyed 32 employers and found 1 non-compliance; the auditors found at least 17 potential violations in the same set. DCWP received only 2 complaints in two years — [DLA Piper](https://knowledge.dlapiper.com/dlapiperknowledge/globalemploymentlatestdevelopments/2026/New-York-Critical-audit-of-New-York-Citys-AI-hiring-law-signals-increased-risk-for-employers); [auditll144.com](https://auditll144.com/blog/ll144-enforcement-state-2026.html)
- DCWP generally agreed with the 13 recommendations: better complaint routing, cross-trained staff, and a move from complaint-driven to proactive review, with an implementation report due within 180 days. No 2026 DCWP penalty actions or rule changes were found. Penalties remain up to $1,500 per violation per day — [Warden AI](https://www.warden-ai.com/resources/nyc-ai-hiring-law-news); [DLA Piper](https://knowledge.dlapiper.com/dlapiperknowledge/globalemploymentlatestdevelopments/2026/New-York-Critical-audit-of-New-York-Citys-AI-hiring-law-signals-increased-risk-for-employers)
- The duty to get a bias audit sits with the **employer/employment agency, not the vendor**. A vendor *may* commission the audit. The law applies where the job is at least part-time in an NYC office, is a remote job tied to an NYC office, or the agency is located in NYC — [DCWP AEDT FAQ](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/about/DCWP-AEDT-FAQ.pdf)
- "Employment agency" is defined by cross-reference to 6 RCNY §5-249. The final rule narrowed AEDT to tools whose "simplified output" is the sole factor, one of a set of factors, or overrules human judgment. DCWP's FAQ says the law does **not** apply when an AEDT is used to scan a resume bank, do outreach to potential candidates, or invite applications — [Perkins Coie](https://www.perkinscoie.com/en/news-insights/nyc-adopts-final-rules-for-law-governing-automated-employment-decision-tools.html); [DCWP FAQ](https://www.nyc.gov/assets/dca/downloads/pdf/about/DCWP-AEDT-FAQ.pdf)

**Illinois**
- HB 3773 (amending the IL Human Rights Act) has been effective since 1 Jan 2026. It bars AI use that has a discriminatory effect, and zip code may not be used as a proxy. Employers must give notice — [Seyfarth (Jun 8 2026)](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/illinois-department-of-human-rights-temporarily-withdraws-proposed-rules-on-use-of-artificial-intelligence-in-employment.html)
- IDHR proposed notice rules (44 Ill. Adm. Code 2520) on 15 May 2026, then **temporarily withdrew** them (per Seyfarth, June 2026). The draft required the notice to name the AI product and its developer/vendor, defined "use" broadly ("AI output influences or facilitates" a covered decision), and added notice to bargaining representatives — [Seyfarth](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/illinois-department-of-human-rights-temporarily-withdraws-proposed-rules-on-use-of-artificial-intelligence-in-employment.html); [Epstein Becker](https://www.ebglaw.com/workforce-bulletin/illinois-proposed-notice-rules-for-complying-with-workplace-ai-anti-discrimination-law); [Ogletree](https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/illinois-steps-up-ai-regulation-in-employment-key-takeaways-for-employers/). Conflict: EBG treats the rules as live and open for comment, while Seyfarth (later) says withdrawn.
- HB 3773 does not require bias audits; the core duty is notice — [Ogletree](https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/illinois-steps-up-ai-regulation-in-employment-key-takeaways-for-employers/)
- **AI Video Interview Act (820 ILCS 42)** [BK]: before AI analysis of video interviews, the employer must give notice, explain how the AI works, and get consent. Videos may be shared only with those whose expertise is needed and must be deleted within 30 days of a request. Employers relying solely on AI to decide on in-person interviews must report demographics — [ILGA text](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/ilcs3.asp?ActID=4015&ChapterID=68)
- **BIPA**: *Deyerler v. HireVue* (filed Jan 2022, Cook County, removed to N.D. Ill.) is a BIPA class action over facial geometry in video interviews. A settlement agreement dated "June __, 2026" appears on classaction.org, but approval was not confirmed — [classaction.org settlement PDF](https://www.classaction.org/media/hirevue-settlement_1.pdf); [complaint](https://www.classaction.org/media/deyerler-v-hirevue-inc.pdf). [BK] The 2024 BIPA amendment (SB 2979) limits recovery to one violation per person per collection method.

**California**
- **FEHA ADS regulations** (Civil Rights Council) took effect 1 Oct 2025. They define an employer's **"agent"** as anyone acting on the employer's behalf, directly or indirectly, "to exercise a function traditionally exercised by the employer or any other FEHA-regulated activity," including recruitment, applicant screening, hiring, promotion, and pay/benefit/leave decisions. They also require 4-year record retention of ADS data — [Sheppard Mullin](https://www.sheppard.com/insights/blogs/california-approves-rules-regulating-ai-in-employment-decision-making); [Littler](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/california-approves-landmark-ai-employment-regulations)
- Vendors that own or license an ADS may be agents. Paul Hastings reads the rules to contemplate vendor liability where a decision-making function is delegated to the vendor. Hinshaw says liability can extend to third-party vendors and staffing agencies. Littler cites *Raines* (an agent of a 5+ employee employer can be directly liable under FEHA) — [Paul Hastings](https://www.paulhastings.com/insights/client-alerts/new-california-regulations-on-employers-use-of-ai-to-make-decisions-go-into-effect-oct-1-2025); [Hinshaw](https://www.hinshawlaw.com/en/insights/blogs/employment-law-observer/employers-ensure-you-are-compliant-with-californias-new-ai-anti-discrimination-rules-under-the-feha); [Littler](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/california-approves-landmark-ai-employment-regulations)
- Evidence of anti-bias testing (quality, scope, recency, results, response) is relevant to defenses — [DLA Piper](https://www.dlapiper.com/insights/publications/2025/10/california-ai-employment-regulations-take-effect)
- **CCPA ADMT regulations** (CPPA): businesses already using ADMT for "significant decisions" (including employment) must comply by **1 Jan 2027**, and new uses must comply before launch. Duties include a pre-use notice (which can be folded into the notice at collection), an opt-out (with a human-appeal exception), access rights about the ADMT logic, and risk assessments — [Akin Gump](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/alerts/new-california-regulations-regarding-employer-use-of-automated-decision-making-technology-compliance-required-by-january-1-2027); [Littler](https://littler.com/news-analysis/asap/californias-long-awaited-final-regulations-automated-decisionmaking-create-new); [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/cppa-finalizes-rules-admt-risk-assessments-and-cybersecurity-audits-requirements). Conflict on the risk-assessment deadline for pre-existing processing: Inside Privacy says 31 Dec 2027, and White & Case gives other dates, with filings to the CPPA by 1 Apr 2028 — [Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/artificial-intelligence/navigating-californias-new-and-emerging-ai-employment-regulations/)

**Colorado**
- SB 24-205 was **repealed and replaced** by SB 26-189 (signed 14 May 2026), a narrower disclosure/transparency ADMT framework effective **1 Jan 2027** — [Intellisee](https://intellisee.com/legislation/colorado-ai-act-repeal-and-replace-sb-26-189-2026/); [Elevate](https://elevateconsult.com/insights/colorado-ai-act/)
- On 27 Apr 2026 a federal magistrate judge stayed enforcement on the AG's stipulation in **xAI's suit** (First Amendment, dormant Commerce Clause, due process and equal protection claims). DOJ's AI Litigation Task Force intervened. Sources disagree on whether the stay expressly reaches SB 26-189. One report says enforcement waits until 14 days after the ruling on the PI motion, and that xAI's filing against the new law is due ~17 Dec 2026. The fiscal note says enforcement waits for AG rulemaking — [Warden AI](https://www.warden-ai.com/resources/colorado-sb-26-189-enforcement-stay); [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/court-blocks-colorado-ai-law-enforcement-until-14-days-after-xai-ruling/); [Carpe Datum](https://www.carpedatumlaw.com/2026/05/colorados-ai-reset-two-weeks-a-white-house-callout-and-a-pivot-away-from-the-eu-model/). **Superseded:** anything describing Colorado's original "duty of care / impact assessment" model or a 30 Jun 2026 effective date.

**Texas TRAIGA (HB 149)**: effective 1 Jan 2026. It prohibits developing or deploying AI **with intent** to discriminate against a protected class, and disparate impact alone is not enough. There are no employer impact-assessment or disclosure duties, though government entities have disclosure duties. AG enforcement only, with a 36-month regulatory sandbox. The "consumer" definition excludes employment context — [Duane Morris](https://www.duanemorris.com/alerts/texas_new_ai_law_is_now_in_effect_what_employers_need_to_know_about_traiga2_0526.html); [Littler](https://www.littler.com/news-analysis/asap/texas-joins-fray-and-enacts-ai-legislation); [Texas AG](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/consumer-protection/file-consumer-complaint/consumer-ai-rights)

**New Jersey**: Division on Civil Rights rules under the LAD (N.J.A.C. 13:16), effective 15 Dec 2025, make employers potentially liable for "algorithmic discrimination" even when using third-party tools without intent. DCR says AEDT use is not per se discriminatory. There is no AI-specific hiring statute; A2726 (introduced 13 Jan 2026) is in committee — [interviewersignal (secondary)](https://interviewersignal.com/blog/new-jersey-ai-hiring-discrimination-guidance); [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/employment-law-watch/102mqfg/state-ai-hiring-tool-regulations-filling-federal-void/)

**Connecticut**: several trackers report Public Act 26-15 (the "CART Act," signed ~late May 2026). Its employment provision reportedly bars employers, from **1 Oct 2026**, from defending a discrimination claim on the ground that an automated tool made the decision. Disclosure duties phase in through Oct 2027. Details are inconsistent across sources and one tracker says CT has no such law, so verify against the session law — [Sheppard Mullin](https://www.sheppard.com/insights/blogs/the-machine-learning-curve-2026-national-ai-employment-law-updates); [workintelhub](https://workintelhub.com/blogs/ai-hiring-laws-by-state)

**Hub classification precedents**
- *Mobley v. Workday* (N.D. Cal. 3:23-cv-00770-RFL, Judge Rita Lin). [BK, July 2024 order]: the court let the theory proceed that Workday is an **"agent"** of employers liable under Title VII/ADEA/ADA because employers delegated screening to it, but **rejected the "employment agency"** theory because Workday does not "procure" applicants. The nationwide ADEA collective was conditionally certified 16 May 2025, and the opt-in deadline was 7 Mar 2026 — [Warden Watch](https://www.warden-ai.com/warden-watch/litigations/mobley-v-workday-inc). On 22 Jun 2026 the core discrimination claims were allowed to proceed (MTD granted in part and denied in part) — [Duane Morris blog (snippet only)](https://blogs.duanemorris.com/classactiondefense/2026/06/24/california-federal-court-grants-in-part-and-denies-in-part-workdays-motion-to-dismiss-in-mobley-v-workday/). On **10 Nov 2026 Workday moved to decertify** the collective, reported at >15,000 opt-ins. No ruling yet. A class-cert hearing on 9 Mar 2027 is reported by a single source — [aigovernanceforhr](https://www.aigovernanceforhr.com/p/the-mobley-v-workday-case-didnt-end-it-escalated); [noova](https://www.noova.global/blog/mobley-v-workday/); [legalanalysis.org](https://www.legalanalysis.org/workday-lawsuit)

### Inferences
- A hub that **procures and refers candidates to employers** (sources applicants, runs matching, delivers shortlists) is closer to the classic "employment agency" definition (Title VII §701(c): one who "regularly undertakes... to procure employees for an employer or to procure for employees opportunities to work") than Workday was. *Mobley* rejected that theory only because Workday did not procure. A matching hub that actively introduces seekers to employers may **not** get the same answer. Under FEHA's broad "agent" definition, ranking or screening on an employer's behalf is the trigger. Under LL144, an NYC-based hub could itself be an "employment agency" that *uses* an AEDT, and would then owe its own bias audit and notices.
- The safest architecture is a **"neutral exchange"**. Each side's own agent applies its principal's criteria. The hub does only deterministic, principal-specified hard-constraint matching (e.g., work authorization, location, salary floor ≥ posted range) and does not compute hub-owned fit scores or rankings. Even then, "simplified output used as one of a set of factors" could catch hub-computed match scores under LL144.
- Because employer-agent behavior happens *on* the hub, plaintiffs will likely name the hub as a co-defendant (as in Mobley and Eightfold). Indemnities and audit logs are essential either way.

### Gaps
- No 2026 primary DCWP enforcement record was found. The 180-day implementation report (due ~June 2026) was not located.
- The exact content of the 22 Jun 2026 Mobley order (which claims were dismissed; any ruling on HiredScore or state claims) could not be read because the host was blocked.
- Connecticut PA 26-15 details and the current IDHR rule status are unverified against primary text.
- No case or regulator has addressed whether a two-sided *agent* marketplace (rather than an ATS or screening vendor) is an employment agency.

## Q2. Federal posture: preemption efforts, EEOC, FTC (2025–2026)

### Takeaway
The federal government has moved from enforcing against algorithmic bias to **trying to preempt state AI laws and dropping disparate-impact enforcement**. But no statute has preempted anything, Title VII disparate impact is still law for private plaintiffs, and state laws remain in force unless a court enjoins them. Plan for state compliance, plus private litigation as the main enforcement channel.

### Cited Findings
- **EO 14365** (11 Dec 2025) directed the AG to create an **AI Litigation Task Force**, created ~9–10 Jan 2026. Its first concrete move was intervening in xAI v. Colorado (Apr 2026). As of 13 Nov 2026 one tracker found no standalone Task Force suit against any other state, including California. Commerce missed its 11 Mar 2026 deadline for the state-law evaluation — [Paul Hastings](https://www.paulhastings.com/insights/client-alerts/president-trump-signs-executive-order-challenging-state-ai-laws); [ailawsbystate](https://www.ailawsbystate.com/blog/federal-ai-preemption-doj-task-force-state-law-fight-2026); [Latham](https://www.lw.com/en/insights/ai-executive-order-targets-state-laws-and-seeks-uniform-federal-standards)
- The White House sent a National AI Policy Framework with preemption recommendations to Congress on 20 Mar 2026. It was not enacted, and commentary says Congress has twice declined to preempt (including the 2025 reconciliation moratorium attempt). A further EO on ~2 Jun 2026 reportedly reinforced the strategy — [Ropes & Gray](https://www.ropesgray.com/en/insights/alerts/2026/03/the-white-house-legislative-recommendations-national-policy-framework-for-artificial-intelligence-an); [fedlaws.org](https://fedlaws.org/ai-executive-order-summary-eos-14179-14365-and-june-2026/)
- **FTC proposed policy statement** (1 Jul 2026), "Suppression of Accuracy in AI Systems," issued under EO 14365. It says intentionally steering AI outputs away from what consumers reasonably expect, including to comply with state AI laws, may be **deceptive under Section 5** unless clearly disclosed, and that compliance with state law is no defense. It names Colorado SB 26-189 and asserts implied conflict preemption. It is non-binding, and comments closed ~31 Jul 2026 — [Arnold & Porter](https://www.arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/blogs/major-questions-an-administrative-law-and-regulatory-blog/2026/07/ftcs-proposed-policy-statement-on-ai-accuracy-and-preemption-of-state-ai-laws); [Spencer Fane](https://www.spencerfane.com/insight/ftc-proposes-new-policy-on-ai-accuracy-hiding-how-an-ai-system-is-steered-may-violate-federal-law/); [TechPolicy.Press (critical)](https://www.techpolicy.press/the-ftc-statement-on-ai-bias-lacks-conviction/)
- **EEOC**: EO 14281 (23 Apr 2025) told agencies to deprioritize disparate impact. The EEOC's AI technical-assistance documents were removed on 27 Jan 2025. The 4 Jun 2026 National Enforcement Plan reportedly does not mention AI and says the agency will not litigate disparate-impact claims. A reported 9 Jun 2026 DOJ OLC opinion calls Title VII disparate-impact enforcement unconstitutional (secondary report, non-binding) — [NatLawReview](https://natlawreview.com/article/federal-government-quietly-removed-its-ai-hiring-guidance-four-states-are-writing); [techjacksolutions (secondary)](https://techjacksolutions.com/ai-brief/doj-olc-declares-eeoc-disparate-impact-guidelines/); [Reed Smith](https://www.reedsmith.com/our-insights/blogs/employment-law-watch/102mqfg/state-ai-hiring-tool-regulations-filling-federal-void/)
- Title VII, the ADEA and the ADA are unchanged. Private disparate-impact suits continue, and *Mobley* is the example — [interviewersignal](https://interviewersignal.com/blog/eeoc-guidance-on-ai-in-hiring)
- **EEOC v. iTutorGroup** [BK]: settled Aug 2023 for $365,000. Software auto-rejected women 55+ and men 60+. This was the first EEOC AI-hiring settlement and was an intentional-discrimination (disparate treatment) case — [EEOC press release](https://www.eeoc.gov/newsroom/itutorgroup-pay-365000-settle-eeoc-discriminatory-hiring-suit)
- **FTC Section 5 AI enforcement**: "Operation AI Comply" (Sep 2024: DoNotPay, Rytr and others) continued under the new administration, e.g., Workado's "98% accurate" AI-detection claim (order finalized 2025). Unsubstantiated AI capability claims are treated as deceptive — [Benesch](https://www.beneschlaw.com/insight/one-year-in-ftcs-operation-ai-comply-continues-under-new-administration-signaling-enduring-enforcement-focus/); [casrai](https://casrai.org/guides/ai-washing-enforcement-sec-ftc-record)

### Inferences
- Deceptive or disparate-treatment conduct is still squarely enforceable federally (FTC Section 5; EEOC disparate treatment like iTutorGroup). Hub marketing claims such as "bias-free matching," "verified candidates" or "x% match accuracy" need substantiation.
- The FTC accuracy statement creates a **tension**. If the hub tunes agent outputs (e.g., suppresses protected attributes) for state-law compliance, it should *disclose* that the system does this. That satisfies the FTC's disclosure safe harbor and costs little.
- Federal preemption is a litigation risk to *state laws*, not a compliance reprieve. Assume CA, IL, NYC and NJ remain enforceable through 2027.

### Gaps
- The Federal Register text of the June 2026 EO and the OLC memo were not verified.
- Whether the FTC finalized the accuracy policy statement after the comment period is unknown.

## Q3. EU AI Act: Annex III employment, Digital Omnibus delay, provider vs deployer duties for a hub

### Takeaway
Recruitment and candidate-evaluation AI is Annex III high-risk. Its obligations now apply from **2 Dec 2027** (Reg. (EU) 2026/1744). Article 50 transparency duties (telling people they are interacting with AI) are **not** delayed. A hub that supplies the matching/ranking engine is a **provider** of a high-risk system. Employers using it are **deployers**. A neutral transport layer whose agents belong to the users is not a provider, but the line is thin.

### Cited Findings
- **Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744**: published in the OJ 24 Jul 2026, in force 27 Jul 2026 (EP vote 16 Jun, Council 29 Jun, signed 8 Jul). Annex III stand-alone high-risk obligations moved from 2 Aug 2026 to **2 Dec 2027**, and Annex I (product-embedded) to **2 Aug 2028**. It adds a prohibition on AI generating non-consensual intimate content/CSAM and converts the AI-literacy duty into a promotion duty for the Commission and Member States — [regulation-ai.eu](https://www.regulation-ai.eu/en/omnibus/); [AGORÀ](https://agora-intelligence.com/en/blog/eu-digital-omnibus-ai-act-deadlines-deferred/); [Jones Walker](https://www.joneswalker.com/en/insights/blogs/ai-law-blog/yes-august-2-still-matters-the-eu-approved-a-high-risk-ai-delay-but-most-trans.html); [Gibson Dunn (snippet)](https://www.gibsondunn.com/eu-ai-act-omnibus-agreement-postponed-high-risk-deadlines-and-other-key-changes/). **Superseded:** any source stating that employment high-risk duties apply 2 Aug 2026 (e.g., the [Eversheds](https://www.eversheds-sutherland.com/en/global/insights/eu-ai-act-high-risk-ai-systems-in-employment) piece).
- Article 50 transparency duties largely still applied from **2 Aug 2026**. Sources conflict on grace periods (e.g., until 2 Dec 2026 for some content-marking duties) — [Jones Walker](https://www.joneswalker.com/en/insights/blogs/ai-law-blog/yes-august-2-still-matters-the-eu-approved-a-high-risk-ai-delay-but-most-trans.html); [cruxdigits](https://cruxdigits.nl/eu-ai-act-high-risk-deadline/)
- **Annex III point 4(a)** [BK, text]: "AI systems intended to be used for the recruitment or selection of natural persons, in particular to place targeted job advertisements, to analyse and filter job applications, and to evaluate candidates." Point 4(b) covers promotion/termination, task allocation based on behaviour or traits, and monitoring/evaluation — [AI Act Annex III](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/annex/3/)
- Draft Commission high-risk guidelines (June 2026) reportedly give an **automated job-matching and ranking tool that scores/shortlists candidates** as an Annex III(4) example, and read "employment" broadly to include freelancers and contractors — [Faegre Drinker (snippet only)](https://www.faegredrinker.com/zh/insights/publications/2026/6/draft-commission-guidelines-on-high-risk-ai-implications-for-the-workplace)
- **Deployer duties (Art. 26)**: use per the instructions, assign human oversight, ensure input data under the deployer's control is relevant and representative, keep logs for ≥6 months, monitor, and inform workers and their representatives before workplace deployment. Deployer fines reach €15M or 3% of turnover — [Eversheds](https://www.eversheds-sutherland.com/en/global/insights/eu-ai-act-high-risk-ai-systems-in-employment); [Art. 26](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/26/)
- [BK] Art. 25: a deployer or distributor becomes a **provider** if it puts its name/trademark on a high-risk system, substantially modifies it, or changes its intended purpose so it becomes high-risk. Art. 6(3) exempts systems that perform only narrow procedural or preparatory tasks without materially influencing decisions, but never systems that profile natural persons — [Art. 25](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/25/); [Art. 6](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/6/)
- Staffing intermediaries are discussed as both providers and deployers depending on the role — [artificialintelligenceact.eu staffing explainer](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/what-the-act-means-for-staffing-businesses/)

### Inferences
- Hub as **provider**: if the hub supplies hiring-matching logic (ranking, scoring, targeted job-ad distribution), it needs a QMS, risk management, data governance, technical documentation, logging, human-oversight design, accuracy/robustness, conformity assessment (internal control for Annex III(4)), CE marking, EU database registration and post-market monitoring. Profiling of candidates rules out the Art. 6(3) escape.
- "Targeted job advertisements" in Annex III(4)(a) means even pushing job *offers* to selected agents can be high-risk. That reaches the hub's core "match needs/offers" function in the hiring vertical.
- A **general-purpose** hub could split itself: a non-high-risk generic messaging/discovery layer, plus a hiring module treated as a high-risk system (or kept rule-based and principal-driven). Article 50 applies now: agents must disclose to natural persons that they are AI when they hand off to humans.

### Gaps
- The final Commission high-risk guidelines (Art. 6) and harmonised standards (CEN-CENELEC JTC 21) were not confirmed as of Nov 2026.
- How the Act treats *users' own* agents (each user a provider/deployer of their own agent?) in a multi-agent marketplace is not addressed in any guidance found.

## Q4. Data protection for agent-to-agent personal-data exchange (GDPR, AEPD, EDPB, ICO, UK)

### Takeaway
EU regulators say autonomy does not move responsibility. Each principal (or its agent provider) is a controller for what its agent does. The hub is probably an **independent controller** for its own matching, trust and safety, and logs, and potentially a **joint controller** with each side where it co-determines the purposes of the exchange. UK regulators are focused on **solely automated recruitment decisions** and want meaningful human involvement.

### Cited Findings
- **AEPD agentic AI guidance** (18 Feb 2026, the first EU DPA guidance on agentic AI; 71 or 81 pages per different sources). Agents are technical means of processing, not legal actors, and autonomy does not alter controller/processor allocation. The controller must document data flows, the third parties and their role at each step, and the agent's autonomy level. DPIA review is needed when agents change the risk. Agentic AI does not automatically trigger Art. 22. People must be told if agents add new recipients, change retention, or trigger new automated decisions or transfers. A "rule of 2" heuristic (reported by commentators): no agent should combine untrusted input + access to sensitive data + autonomous action affecting individuals without human oversight — [A&O Shearman](https://www.aoshearman.com/en/insights/ao-shearman-on-data/aepd-publishes-guidance-on-agentic-artificial-intelligence-from-a-data-protection-perspective); [Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/artificial-intelligence/spanish-supervisory-authority-issues-detailed-guidance-on-agentic-ai-and-gdpr-compliance/); [Linklaters](https://techinsights.linklaters.com/post/102mk6z/agentic-ai-and-data-protection-guidance-from-the-spanish-aepd); [ECIJA](https://www.ecija.com/en/news-and-insights/agentes-de-ia-y-tratamiento-de-datos-personales-las-claves-de-la-nueva-guia-de-la-aepd/)
- On 27 Oct 2026 the AEPD issued a 10-point guide for **individuals** who let AI agents act for them — [PPC Land](https://ppc.land/spains-aepd-issues-10-point-guide-for-people-who-let-ai-agents-act-for-them/)
- **EDPB**: no agent-specific guidance was found. Related documents: Guidelines 03/2026 on web scraping for gen-AI (v1.0 adopted 7 Jul 2026; consultation closed 30 Oct 2026), the Dec 2024 AI-models opinion, and EDPB-EDPS Joint Opinion 2/2026 on the Digital Omnibus (which touches the controller/processor split) — [EDPB Guidelines 03/2026](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/system/files/2026-07/edpb_guidelines_2020603_webscraping_v1_en_0.pdf); [praxikon](https://www.praxikon.com/en/richtsnoeren/edpb-edps-joint-opinion-2-2026-on-the-proposal-for-a-regulation-as-reg). Bird & Bird flags controllership **shifting mid-process** as the key agentic pressure point and recommends mapping each actor at each step — [Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2026/two-eras,-one-rulebook-what-agentic-ai-does-to-your-gdpr-compliance). The Irish DPC published an AI insights report in Sep 2026 — [DPC](https://www.dataprotection.ie/sites/default/files/uploads/2026-09/DPC-AI-Insights-Report-AC.pdf)
- **ICO (UK)**: report and draft guidance on ADM in recruitment (31 Mar 2026), based on 30+ employers (Mar 2025–Jan 2026). It found many employers likely make **solely automated** decisions without meaningful human involvement. It expects contestability, human intervention, meaningful human review and multi-stage transparency. Consultation ran to ~29 May 2026 — [DLA Piper Privacy Matters](https://privacymatters.dlapiper.com/2026/04/uk-ico-report-on-automated-decision-making-in-recruitment/); [ICO jobseeker blog](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/03/here-s-what-jobseekers-need-to-know-about-automated-recruitment-decisions/); [Brodies](https://brodies.com/insights/ip-technology-and-data/automated-decision-making-in-recruitment-what-the-icos-latest-report-means-for-employers/)
- ICO Tech Futures report on agentic AI (Jan 2026) is explicitly *not* guidance. It flags future updates to ADM and profiling guidance for agents — [Skadden-hosted PDF](https://www.skadden.com/-/media/files/publications/2026/03/uk-regulator-to-agentic-ai-developers/tech_futures_report_on_agentic_ai.pdf?rev=dc2775c2950d49b4903362df747ee1e8&hash=6ACF99C92081B932282747997BDFAAAE); [NAVEX](https://www.navex.com/en-us/blog/article/ico-report-agentic-ai-opportunities-and-risks/)
- UK Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 relaxes the old Art. 22 near-prohibition on solely automated decisions, except for special-category data, but keeps the safeguards (information, contest, human intervention) — [Keystone Law](https://keystonelaw.com/keynotes/what-does-the-icos-warning-against-automated-decision-making-mean-for-ai-recruitment-and-employers/)

### Inferences
- Likely role map (uncertain):
  - Job seeker → own agent provider: the provider is a processor for the seeker, or an independent controller if it uses the data for its own purposes.
  - Employer → hiring agent vendor: processor for the employer.
  - Hub: independent controller for account, KYC, matching index, reputation and abuse logs. It may be a **joint controller** (Art. 26 GDPR) with each principal for the exchange if it defines the exchange schema and match purposes. That calls for an Art. 26 arrangement in the ToS plus transparency to data subjects.
  - Once data is disclosed to the counterparty, the counterparty is a new independent controller. The hub should model each disclosure as a recipient event visible to the data subject.
- Data minimisation suggests **staged disclosure**: anonymous capability and constraint matching first, then identity and contact details only after both principals opt in. Special-category data (health, ethnicity, union membership) should be barred from agent payloads by schema.
- Cross-border transfers (US hub, EU users) need the EU-US DPF or SCCs. DPF stability was not re-checked this session.

### Gaps
- No EDPB/CNIL guidance specifically on agent-to-agent exchange was found. No DPA enforcement against an agent marketplace was found.
- The status of the EU Digital Omnibus GDPR amendments (separate from the AI omnibus) was not verified.

## Q5. Agency/contract law, platform liability (Section 230), FTC Act, FCRA

### Takeaway
Agents' commitments are generally **attributed to the principal who deployed them** (UETA §14, E-SIGN §7001(h)), but whether this extends to non-deterministic LLM agents is untested. **Section 230 is unlikely to protect hub-generated or hub-shaped content and matching criteria**, and the main risk-limiter is to keep the hub a conduit for user-agent content. **FCRA** is a live litigation theory against AI hiring platforms that assemble third-party data and score candidates (*Kistler v. Eightfold*).

### Cited Findings
- **UETA §14**: contracts can form through the interaction of electronic agents even without human review. Per the official comment, intent "flows from the programming and use of the machine." E-SIGN parallels this, and agent action must be "legally attributable to the person to be bound." UETA §10 lets an individual avoid an error made in dealing with another person's electronic agent if no error-correction opportunity was provided — [Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/contract-law-in-the-age-of-agentic-ai-whos-really-clicking-accept); [E-SIGN 15 U.S.C. §7001(h)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/7001)
- No court has resolved whether §14 covers LLM agents that exercise judgment. Commentators say UETA was written for deterministic programs, and it does not settle term enforceability — [Proskauer](https://newmedialaw.proskauer.com/2025/04/09/contract-law-in-the-age-of-agentic-ai-whos-really-clicking-accept/); [promise.legal](https://blog.promise.legal/ai-agent-legal-liability-contracting-authority/)
- [BK] Restatement (Third) of Agency §1.04 cmt. e: a computer program is not an agent (it lacks legal personality), so liability runs to the user via attribution, not agency. *Moffatt v. Air Canada* (BC CRT 2024): the company was liable for its chatbot's misrepresentation of the bereavement-fare policy, and the "separate legal entity" argument was rejected — [CanLII 2024 BCCRT 149](https://www.canlii.org/en/bc/bccrt/doc/2024/2024bccrt149/2024bccrt149.html)
- **Section 230**: no appellate ruling on LLM output. In *Bouck v. Meta* (N.D. Cal., Mar 2026) the court said Meta's gen-AI ad-copy tools may make it a "creator," so §230 did not apply, though the case was later dismissed on securities preemption. *Suddeth v. Meta* applied §230 to algorithmic amplification claims — [Eric Goldman blog](https://blog.ericgoldman.org/archives/2026/06/section-230-doesnt-apply-to-generative-ai-enhancements-to-ad-copy-but-the-plaintiffs-lose-anyway-bouck-and-suddeth-v-meta.htm); [Crowell](https://www.crowell.com/en/insights/client-alerts/the-pipe-not-the-posts-how-section-230s-protections-extend-to-generative-ai-platforms). As of 6 Nov 2026, no §230 ruling had been made in the coordinated OpenAI chatbot cases. *Garcia v. Character Technologies* settled Jan 2026 and is often mis-cited on §230 — [ailawsuittracker](https://ailawsuittracker.com/issues/defamation/)
- [BK] *Fair Housing Council v. Roommates.com* (9th Cir. en banc 2008): a site lost §230 immunity for discriminatory matching because it *required* users to answer questions about protected characteristics and matched on them ("material contribution"). Neutral tools stayed protected — [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_Housing_Council_of_San_Fernando_Valley_v._Roommates.com,_LLC)
- **FCRA**: *Kistler v. Eightfold AI* was filed 20 Jan 2026 in Contra Costa Superior Court, removed to N.D. Cal. ~2 Mar 2026, and is listed as No. 3:26-cv-1768. It alleges undisclosed AI "match scores" built from third-party data are consumer reports (FCRA + CA ICRAA). The MTD was fully briefed by 9 Jul 2026. No ruling was confirmed as of the last verified filing (24 Sep 2026). It makes no bias theory, only process — [Ogletree](https://ogletree.com/insights-resources/blog-posts/groundbreaking-lawsuit-tests-whether-ai-hiring-tools-trigger-fcra-compliance/); [Akin](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/ai-law-and-regulation-tracker/ai-hiring-platform-faces-fcra-class-action-over-data-use-or-kistler-et-al-v-eightfold-ai-inc); [settlementinsight](https://settlementinsight.com/eightfold-ai-lawsuit); [Fisher Phillips](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/insights/insights/job-applicants-sue-ai-screening-company-for-fcra-violations)
- [BK] CFPB Circular 2024-06 (Oct 2024) said background dossiers and algorithmic worker scores can be consumer reports. The CFPB withdrew numerous guidance documents in May 2025, and whether this circular was among them should be verified — [CFPB circular](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/circulars/consumer-financial-protection-circular-2024-06-background-dossiers-and-algorithmic-scores-for-hiring-promotion-and-other-employment-decisions/)
- **FTC Section 5**: deceptive AI claims are actionable ("a deceptive claim made by an AI agent is still a deceptive claim"). Enforcement against "accurately described but autonomously harmful" agents is unsettled — [techjacksolutions](https://techjacksolutions.com/ai-brief/ftc-operation-ai-comply-cox-media-active-listening-930k/); [Benesch](https://www.beneschlaw.com/insight/one-year-in-ftcs-operation-ai-comply-continues-under-new-administration-signaling-enduring-enforcement-focus/)

### Inferences
- **Binding commitments**: the hub should treat agent-to-agent "agreements" as non-binding term sheets by default. It should require an explicit human confirmation step (a UETA "security procedure" and §10 error-correction opportunity) before anything binding (offers, acceptance, contact release). Authority scopes ("mandates") should be machine-readable and enforced by the hub.
- **Misrepresentation**: the principal is liable for its agent's statements (*Moffatt*). The hub can be liable if it *generates* or *rewrites* content, since §230 likely does not cover LLM generation (Bouck), and if its schema solicits protected traits (Roommates). So: never have hub models author claims on a principal's behalf, and keep a neutral schema.
- **FCRA trigger**: if the hub enriches candidate profiles with third-party data (scraped profiles, background or reputation scores) and furnishes scores to employers for hiring, it risks being a CRA (permissible purpose, accuracy, adverse-action notices, disputes). Avoid hub-computed candidate scores from third-party data. Reputation should be based on first-party conduct on the hub only, or the hub should formally operate as a CRA.

### Gaps
- No court decision applies UETA §14 or agency law to LLM agents. No §230 decision on agent-to-agent platforms was found.
- The Eightfold MTD outcome is unknown.

## Q6. Trust & safety incidents and enforcement/litigation lessons

### Takeaway
The first open agent network (Moltbook) failed on every basic T&S axis: credential exposure, no proof the agents were AI or bound to a principal, bot-to-bot prompt injection, and crypto scams. Hiring already suffers sybil floods (auto-apply) and identity fraud (deepfake candidates, fake recruiters). An agent hub will concentrate these attacks unless identity, rate limits and injection-hardening are built in.

### Cited Findings
- **Moltbook breach** (disclosed ~31 Jan–2 Feb 2026 by Wiz). A Supabase key in client JavaScript, with **Row Level Security disabled**, gave full DB read/write: ~1.5M agent API tokens, ~35k emails (Wiz counted 29,631 early-access emails), and private agent messages, some containing plaintext third-party keys (e.g., OpenAI). Anyone could post or act as any agent. It was fixed within hours, with no evidence of prior exploitation — [Wiz](https://www.wiz.io/pt-br/blog/exposed-moltbook-database-reveals-millions-of-api-keys); [SiliconANGLE](https://siliconangle.com/2026/02/02/ai-agent-social-network-moltbook-left-millions-credentials-publicly-exposed/); [Infosecurity](https://infosecurity-magazine.com/news/moltbook-exposes-user-data-api)
- Wiz: the 1.5M "agents" had only ~17,000 human owners, and there was no way to verify an agent was AI rather than a human with a script — [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/security/ai-agent-social-media-network-moltbook-is-a-security-disaster-millions-of-credentials-and-other-details-left-unsecured)
- **Bot-to-bot prompt injection** (Permiso via SecurityWeek): agents instructed to inject other agents included pushing them to delete their accounts, crypto pump schemes ("SuskBot," rated critical), false-authority claims and jailbreak spreading. A malicious ClawHub skill ("bob-p2p") was promoted on Moltbook in an active crypto scam. Vectra: ~2.6% of sampled posts carried hidden injection payloads. Another assessment counted 506 injection attacks in the first 72h. Tenable described the platform as dominated by spam and scams — [SecurityWeek](https://www.securityweek.com/security-analysis-of-moltbook-agent-network-bot-to-bot-prompt-injection-and-data-leaks/); [SC World](https://www.scworld.com/brief/novel-ai-agent-powered-crypto-scam-uncovered); [Vectra](https://vectra.ai/blog/moltbook-and-the-illusion-of-harmless-ai-agent-communities); [Tenable](https://www.tenable.com/blog/undercover-on-moltbook); [IST lessons](https://securityandtechnology.org/blog/lessons-from-moltbook-when-agents-talk-to-agents/)
- **Hiring fraud and sybils**: auto-apply services send thousands of applications. Pin's 2026 survey of 420+ recruiters: 22.4% confirmed a deepfake or identity swap in a video interview, and another 16.3% suspected one. Greenhouse 2026: 91% of recruiters had spotted or suspected candidate deception. Gartner projects 1 in 4 applicants fake by 2028 (a forecast). Fake-recruiter scams use voice cloning, and one source cites ~$13M in FBI IC3 AI-involved employment-scam losses. StepStone (Nov 2026): 73% of recruiters are more sceptical. Many figures come from vendors — [Pin](https://www.pin.com/blog/deepfake-candidate-report/); [cumberlink](https://cumberlink.com/exclusive/article_f4ddc3f4-7b1b-5848-83aa-be576e09d838.html); [NYSBA](https://nysba.org/addressing-the-threat-of-fake-job-candidates/); [ad-hoc-news (StepStone)](https://ad-hoc-news.de/boerse/news/corporate-news/ai-assisted-deception-in-job-applications-four-in-ten-recruiters-have-detected-or-suspect-its-use/70519972)
- **AI-hiring litigation/enforcement roll-up**: *Mobley v. Workday* (agent theory; collective >15k; decert motion 10 Nov 2026); *Kistler v. Eightfold* (FCRA); *Deyerler v. HireVue* (BIPA; settlement doc mid-2026); ACLU charges vs Intuit and HireVue (Mar 2025, Colorado CRD + EEOC: deaf Indigenous employee denied captioning accommodation in an AI interview; HireVue says no AI assessment was used; no outcome found); EEOC v. iTutorGroup ($365k, 2023) — [Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/another-legal-challenge-to-an-ai-interviewing-tool); [Fisher Phillips](https://www.fisherphillips.com/en/news-insights/ai-screening-systems-face-fresh-scrutiny-6-key-takeaways-from-claims-filed-against-hiring-technology-company.html); sources above.

### Inferences
- Lessons for the hub:
  1. Moltbook-class failures are configuration bugs, not exotic AI attacks. Use RLS/authz tests, no client-side service keys, secret scanning of agent messages, and a pre-launch pen test.
  2. "Agent count" is a vanity and sybil metric. Bind every agent to a verified principal (KYC/KYB) and rate-limit per principal.
  3. Every counterparty message is untrusted input. Use typed schemas, strip or quarantine free text, never execute tool instructions from counterparties, and apply the AEPD "rule of 2."
  4. Skills/plugins are a supply-chain vector (bob-p2p), so allowlist and sandbox them.
  5. Hiring is a high-fraud domain on both sides (fake candidates and fake recruiters). Verified-employer badges and liveness/identity checks at human hand-off are needed.
- The accessibility angle (ACLU/HireVue) means agent-mediated flows must offer accommodations and a human alternative channel (ADA).

### Gaps
- Permiso's original report was not opened. No regulator action against Moltbook was found. No public incident yet involves a hiring-specific agent marketplace.

## Q7. Fairness: unequal agent capability, algorithmic discrimination, pay transparency

### Takeaway
Agent-vs-agent negotiation systematically advantages principals with stronger models, and the disadvantaged principals **don't notice**. In hiring, that maps onto socioeconomic disparity and could add up to disparate impact. Pay-transparency laws give the hub a natural "fair-terms floor": require posted ranges and forbid agents from negotiating below them.

### Cited Findings
- **Anthropic Project Deal**: a one-week internal marketplace in which 69 employees' Claude agents closed 186 deals across 500+ items (~$4k) with no human sign-off. Four parallel markets ran, two all-Opus 4.5 and two mixing Opus and Haiku, with identical instructions. Across 161 items sold at least twice, Opus agents sold for $2.68 more and bought for $2.45 less on average. Paired items: Opus sellers earned $3.64 more. Haiku-represented humans reported similar satisfaction and fairness despite worse outcomes. Aggressive prompting had no significant effect, and model capability mattered. Anthropic says the setup "doesn't reflect how we think agents should be deployed" — [Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/features/project-deal); [The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/anthropic-says-stronger-ai-models-cut-better-deals-and-the-losers-dont-even-notice/); [EdTech Innovation Hub](https://www.edtechinnovationhub.com/news/anthropics-ai-agents-struck-186-deals-in-a-real-marketplace-and-the-stronger-model-won-every-time). A cited Stanford/MIT study reportedly found weaker buyer agents paid ~2% more and weaker seller agents lost up to 14% profit (secondary, not verified) — [dev.to summary](https://dev.to/techsifted/anthropic-tested-a-marketplace-where-ai-agents-bought-and-sold-real-things-heres-what-they-found-32dd)
- **EU Pay Transparency Directive** (2023/970): the transposition deadline of 7 Jun 2026 passed with most Member States late. On time: Slovakia, Italy, Lithuania and Malta. NL, SE, CZ and DK target 1 Jan 2027. The Commission says there will be no pause or extension — [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2026/06/eu-pay-transparency-directive-the-deadline-for-transposition-has-passed-what-now); [Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2026/07/01/eu-pay-transparency-directive-2026-employer-compliance); [Lockton](https://global.lockton.com/us/en/news-insights/eu-pay-transparency-directive-implementation-status). [BK] The Directive requires pay or an initial range to be given to applicants before interview (Art. 5) and **bans asking candidates about pay history**.
- [BK] US pay-range posting laws (CA, CO, WA, NY State/NYC, IL from 1 Jan 2025, MN from 1 Jan 2025, NJ from Jun 2025, VT from Jul 2025, MA from Oct 2025, among others) require good-faith ranges in postings. Several states ban salary-history questions. This was not re-verified this session.
- [BK] The Annex III and US disparate-impact framing: proxies such as zip code (explicitly barred in IL HB 3773), graduation year (age, which is the *Mobley* ADEA theory) and employment gaps (disability/caregiving) are the usual sources of algorithmic discrimination.

### Inferences
- A hiring agent that asks a candidate's agent for "current salary" may violate salary-history bans in many US jurisdictions and the EU Directive. The hub should **block that field at the schema level** and make "posted range" a mandatory field for employer offers in range-law jurisdictions.
- Capability asymmetry: employers will deploy stronger agents than many job seekers. Mitigations:
  - Hub-provided baseline negotiation agent or "fair-terms" defaults for seekers.
  - Structured offers (range, benefits) in place of open haggling.
  - Outcome dashboards by agent class.
  - A "human review before accept" default for seekers.
  - Periodic disparity monitoring, i.e., the bias-audit-style selection-rate/impact-ratio statistics required by LL144.
- Allowing the hub's own models to infer protected traits is risky. But *not* collecting voluntary demographics makes bias audits impossible. Collect them via a separate, opt-in, firewalled self-ID channel used only for audits.

### Gaps
- No empirical study of capability asymmetry *in hiring negotiations* specifically was found.
- The current list of US pay-transparency states as of Nov 2026 was not re-verified.

## Q8. Concrete compliance-by-design requirements for the hub

### Takeaway
Build the hub as a **neutral, auditable exchange**, not a decision-maker. Principals' agents decide. The hub enforces identity, authority, schema, disclosure staging, human gates and logs. In hiring, it either stays outside the "AEDT/high-risk provider" boundary or crosses it deliberately with an audit program. The requirements below are inferences synthesised from Q1–Q7 and should be validated by counsel.

### Cited Findings
- Anchors already cited above: LL144 bias-audit and notice duties (DCWP FAQ); CA FEHA agent definition and 4-year retention (Sheppard Mullin/Littler); CCPA ADMT notice, opt-out and access by 1 Jan 2027 (Akin Gump); IL HB 3773 notice (Seyfarth); AEPD flow documentation and autonomy levels (A&O Shearman); ICO meaningful human involvement (DLA Piper); AI Act Art. 26 logs ≥6 months and worker information (Eversheds); Art. 50 AI-interaction disclosure (Jones Walker); UETA human-confirmation and security-procedure logic (Proskauer); Moltbook RLS and key-exposure lessons (Wiz); FCRA third-party data scoring risk (Ogletree on Eightfold).

### Inferences — design requirements
**A. Role and scope architecture**
1. Keep **decision-making with principals**. The hub does deterministic, principal-specified hard-constraint filtering only, with no hub-owned candidate scores or rankings in the hiring vertical. Document this "intended purpose" (AI Act) and the "not an AEDT/ADS" position (LL144, FEHA, CCPA ADMT), and re-review whenever a ranking or recommendation feature is added.
2. If ranking/recommendation is offered, **treat it as a high-risk/AEDT product**. Run an annual independent bias audit (LL144 impact ratios by sex and race/ethnicity and intersections), publish a summary, prepare AI Act provider documentation for 2 Dec 2027, and offer employers a compliance pack (notices, audit summary, instructions for use).
3. **Do not procure candidates for employers** (no hub-sourced outreach on employers' behalf) unless the hub is prepared to be an "employment agency." Revisit with counsel per state.
4. **Hiring module ring-fenced** from the general-purpose hub: separate schemas, policies, retention and audit logs.

**B. Identity, authority, anti-abuse**
5. KYC (individuals) / KYB (employers, including domain and EIN/company registry checks). Every agent is cryptographically bound to a verified principal; per-request signing, OAuth, no shared secrets in client config.
6. Machine-readable **mandates**: what each agent may disclose, offer or accept, with hub enforcement of limits and expiry.
7. Per-principal quotas and rate limits. Application caps per seeker per period (anti auto-apply flood). Reputation is tied to the principal and built only from first-party on-hub behaviour (avoids the FCRA CRA trigger).
8. Verified-employer and verified-human badges. Liveness or identity checks at human hand-off (deepfake/fake-recruiter defence).

**C. Agent-to-agent security**
9. Typed negotiation objects. Counterparty free text is labeled untrusted, scanned for injection payloads, and never forwarded as instructions. Apply the AEPD "rule of 2" by default (no untrusted input + sensitive data + autonomous effect without a human gate).
10. Secrets scanning and redaction on all messages (Moltbook leaked third-party keys in DMs). Row-level security plus authz test suites. External pen test before launch. Bug bounty.
11. Plugin/skill allowlist and sandboxing (ClawHub "bob-p2p" lesson). No payments in v1.

**D. Data protection**
12. **Staged disclosure**: anonymous constraint match → mutual opt-in → identity → contact details. Each stage is a logged "recipient event" visible to the data subject (AEPD transparency).
13. Schema-level **prohibited fields**: salary history, age/DOB/graduation year in early stages, photos, health/disability, ethnicity, religion, union status, immigration details beyond yes/no work authorization, zip code as a ranking input (IL HB 3773).
14. A separate, opt-in, firewalled **demographic self-ID** store used only for aggregate bias monitoring.
15. GDPR: publish a controller map. The hub is an independent controller for its operations, with an Art. 26 joint-controller arrangement for the exchange layer where applicable. DPIA for the hiring vertical. SCCs/DPF for transfers. A DSAR flow that covers agent logs.
16. Retention: hiring-related ADS/agent records for ≥4 years where CA FEHA applies, and AI Act logs ≥6 months. Reconcile with GDPR storage limitation by jurisdiction-tagged retention policies.

**E. Human-in-the-loop and transparency**
17. **No binding commitment without human confirmation** (offers, acceptances, contact release, interview scheduling commitments). Provide an error-correction step (UETA §10 analogue).
18. Article 50 / bot disclosure: agents identify as AI to any human. Hand-off messages carry "this summary was AI-generated by X's agent."
19. Candidate-facing notices that employers can reuse: AI use notice (IL HB 3773, including product and vendor naming per the draft rule), LL144 notice (10 business days, job qualifications/characteristics), CCPA ADMT pre-use notice plus opt-out/human-review route from 1 Jan 2027, ICO/UK GDPR contest and human-review rights.
20. Accessibility: a human alternative channel and accommodation requests in every hiring flow (ADA; ACLU/HireVue lesson).

**F. Fairness and pay**
21. Mandatory pay range on employer offers (required in range-law jurisdictions, default elsewhere). Agents cannot request salary history.
22. Capability-parity measures: a hub-provided baseline seeker agent, structured offers, outcome disparity monitoring by agent/model class and by demographic (self-ID), and published aggregate stats.
23. Marketing claims ("bias-free," "verified," "accurate match") only with substantiation (FTC Section 5). Disclose any output steering applied for legal compliance (FTC July 2026 accuracy statement).

**G. Contracts and governance**
24. ToS: employers are deployers/controllers of their agents and responsible for employment decisions, with warranties of compliance with LL144, FEHA, IL and NJ, and indemnities. Seekers accept that agent outputs are non-binding until confirmed. Allocate liability for agent misstatements to principals (*Moffatt*), while recognising that indemnities do not bind regulators or plaintiffs.
25. Jurisdiction gating at launch: consider excluding NYC-located employers, or requiring their LL144 attestations, until the audit program exists. Track Colorado (xAI case, ~Dec 2026 filing), the CCPA ADMT go-live (1 Jan 2027), Connecticut phase-ins (to Oct 2027) and the EU 2 Dec 2027 date.
26. Incident response and abuse reporting: 72h GDPR breach notification readiness, US state breach laws, a public T&S transparency report.

### Gaps
- No regulator has opined on an agent-to-agent hub specifically. Every role allocation above is analogical.
- Insurance market (AI liability / tech E&O for agent marketplaces) was not researched.
- State-by-state "employment agency" licensing (e.g., NY GBL Art. 11, CA Talent Agencies Act analogues) was not researched and may matter if the hub charges job seekers.
